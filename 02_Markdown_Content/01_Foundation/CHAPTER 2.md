**Chapter 2 — Computational Thinking on Problem Solving**

**2.1 Introduction to Computational Thinking**

**2.1.1 What Is Computational Thinking?**

Computational thinking (CT) is a systematic approach to understanding
problems and developing solutions that can be carried out by people or
computers.

It involves analyzing a problem, identifying important information,
recognizing similarities, breaking complex tasks into smaller parts, and
designing logical steps to reach a solution.

Computational thinking does not mean simply thinking like a computer or
writing computer programs. It is a general problem-solving skill that
can be applied in computer science, mathematics, science, business,
education, and everyday life.

Computer scientist Jeannette M. Wing (2006) introduced an influential
discussion of computational thinking as a fundamental skill that should
extend beyond computer science professionals. Her work helped establish
computational thinking as an important concept in modern computing
education.

A commonly taught framework identifies four fundamental techniques:

1.  Decomposition: Breaking a complex problem into smaller, manageable
    parts.

2.  Pattern Recognition: Identifying similarities, repetitions, and
    trends.

3.  Abstraction: Focusing on essential information while ignoring
    unnecessary details.

4.  Algorithm Design: Developing a logical, step-by-step procedure to
    solve a problem.

These techniques work together to help people understand problems and
develop effective solutions.

**2.1.2 Why Is Computational Thinking Important?**

Computational thinking is important because many real-world problems are
too complicated to solve effectively without a structured approach.

Instead of attempting to solve an entire problem at once, computational
thinking provides a way to organize information, simplify the problem,
and develop a solution systematically.

Its importance includes:

- Solving complex problems: Dividing large problems into smaller tasks
  makes them easier to understand and manage.

- Improving logical reasoning: Encourages people to think carefully
  about the order of actions and possible outcomes.

- Developing efficient solutions: Recognizing patterns allows previously
  developed solutions to be reused.

- Supporting programming: Helps programmers understand problems before
  translating solutions into programming languages.

- Reducing errors: Structured problem-solving makes it easier to
  identify missing steps and incorrect assumptions.

- Applying knowledge across disciplines: The same techniques can be used
  in mathematics, engineering, science, business, and everyday
  activities.

For example, consider organizing a university event. The task involves
several responsibilities, including selecting a location, preparing a
budget, inviting participants, arranging equipment, and managing the
schedule.

Trying to manage everything simultaneously can become confusing.
Computational thinking helps organize these responsibilities into
smaller tasks and establish a logical plan.

**2.2 Decomposition**

**2.2.1 What Is Decomposition?**

Decomposition is the process of breaking a large or complex problem into
smaller, more manageable subproblems.

A subproblem is a smaller part of the original problem that can be
understood and solved separately.

The main purpose of decomposition is to reduce complexity. Instead of
trying to understand and solve everything simultaneously, we divide the
problem into smaller tasks and determine how those tasks contribute to
the overall solution.

For example, consider the task of developing a university student
registration system. This is a relatively complex project involving
several responsibilities.

We can decompose it into:

1.  Student information management.

2.  Course information management.

3.  Course registration.

4.  Enrollment validation.

5.  Registration confirmation.

6.  Report generation.

Each component can be designed and developed separately before being
combined into the complete system.

**2.2.2 How Does Decomposition Work?**

A practical decomposition process involves the following steps:

1.  Identify the main problem: Clearly understand the overall objective.

2.  Identify major components: Determine the main tasks required to
    achieve the objective.

3.  Break components into smaller tasks: Divide each major task into
    simpler activities.

4.  Identify dependencies: Determine which tasks must be completed
    before others.

5.  Combine the solutions: Integrate the smaller solutions into one
    complete solution.

The decomposition process can continue until each task is sufficiently
simple to understand and solve.

**2.2.3 Practical Example: Organizing a University Event**

Suppose a university wants to organize a programming competition.

The main problem is:

Organize a successful programming competition.

This problem can be divided into five major components:

| **Major component** | **Smaller tasks** |
|----|----|
| Preparation | Define objectives, select date, establish rules. |
| Registration | Create registration forms, collect participant information. |
| Venue | Reserve rooms, prepare computers, arrange internet access. |
| Competition | Distribute problems, manage time, evaluate submissions. |
| Evaluation | Calculate scores, determine rankings, announce results. |

**2.2.4 Advantages of Decomposition**

- Reduces complexity: Smaller problems are easier to understand.

- Improves organization: Tasks can be arranged into a clear structure.

- Supports teamwork: Different people can work on separate components.

- Simplifies debugging: Errors can be investigated within individual
  components.

- Encourages reuse: Solutions to smaller tasks may be useful in other
  projects.

**2.2.5 Decomposition in Programming**

In programming, decomposition is often used to divide a program into
smaller functions or modules.

For example, a student grade management program could contain separate
functions for:

- Reading student information.

- Calculating average scores.

- Determining grades.

- Displaying results.

Each function performs a specific responsibility. This makes the program
easier to develop, test, maintain, and understand.

**Common Misconception**

Decomposition does not mean dividing a problem randomly.

The smaller tasks must have a meaningful relationship with the original
problem. They must also be organized so that their solutions can
eventually be combined into a complete solution.

**2.3 Pattern Recognition**

**2.3.1 What Is Pattern Recognition?**

Pattern recognition is the process of identifying similarities,
repetitions, relationships, or trends within information or across
different problems.

A pattern is a recurring structure or characteristic that can help us
understand something or predict what may happen next.

When solving problems, recognizing patterns allows us to use existing
knowledge rather than developing an entirely new solution every time.

For example, consider the following sequence:

2, 4, 6, 8, 10, ...

The numbers increase by 2 each time. Recognizing this pattern allows us
to predict that the next number is 12.

Pattern recognition is useful in mathematics, programming, data
analysis, artificial intelligence, and everyday decision-making.

**2.3.2 Types of Patterns**

Several types of patterns are commonly encountered in problem solving.

| **Pattern type** | **Explanation** | **Example** |
|----|----|----|
| Repetition | An action or structure occurs repeatedly. | Repeating a task every day. |
| Numerical | Numbers follow a mathematical relationship. | 3, 6, 9, 12, ... |
| Structural | Different problems share a similar organization. | Several programs require user input and output. |
| Behavioral | An event or behavior occurs under similar conditions. | Increased website traffic during certain hours. |
| Sequential | Events follow a recurring order. | Logging in, selecting an option, and confirming an action. |

**2.3.3 How Does Pattern Recognition Work?**

A simple pattern recognition process involves:

1.  Observe the information: Examine the available data or problems.

2.  Identify similarities: Look for repeated characteristics or
    relationships.

3.  Determine the pattern: Establish the rule that explains the
    similarities.

4.  Apply the pattern: Use the identified rule to solve similar
    problems.

5.  Verify the result: Check whether the pattern works in the situations
    being considered.

**2.3.4 Practical Example: Identifying Repeated Tasks**

Suppose a student wants to calculate the average score of five subjects.

The calculations are:

- Mathematics: 90

- Physics: 85

- Programming: 95

- English: 80

- Statistics: 90

The average is calculated using the same procedure:

``` math
\text{Average} = \frac{\text{Total~scores}}{\text{Number~of~subjects}}
```

The repeated pattern is that every subject score is added to the same
total, and the total is divided by the number of subjects.

Instead of designing a different calculation for each subject, we can
identify one general procedure that works for all five scores.

This is particularly useful in programming because repeated operations
can often be handled using the same algorithm.

**2.3.5 Pattern Recognition in Programming**

Pattern recognition helps programmers identify repeated operations and
reusable structures.

Consider a program that calculates the average score of several
students.

Each student requires the same operations:

1.  Read the student's scores.

2.  Add the scores.

3.  Calculate the average.

4.  Display the result.

The same procedure can be applied to every student.

Recognizing this repetition helps programmers develop general solutions
rather than writing separate instructions for each individual.

**2.3.6 Benefits of Pattern Recognition**

- Reduces repetitive work.

- Helps identify relationships within data.

- Supports the development of reusable algorithms.

- Makes similar problems easier to solve.

- Helps identify trends and make predictions.

**Common Misconception**

Not every similarity represents a meaningful pattern.

A pattern must be supported by the available information. For example,
two events occurring at the same time does not automatically mean that
one caused the other.

Always examine the evidence before applying a recognized pattern.

**2.4 Abstraction**

**2.4.1 What Is Abstraction?**

Abstraction is the process of focusing on the essential information
required to solve a problem while ignoring details that are unnecessary
for the current objective.

In simple terms, abstraction means concentrating on what matters.

Real-world problems often contain large amounts of information. However,
not all of that information is relevant to the solution.

Abstraction helps reduce this complexity by creating a simplified
representation of the problem.

For example, a map does not display every tree, building interior, or
person in a city. Instead, it emphasizes useful information such as
roads, locations, and landmarks.

This simplified representation makes navigation easier.

**2.4.2 Why Is Abstraction Important?**

Without abstraction, solving problems would require examining too much
information.

Consider a university application that calculates student grades.

The system may need:

- Student identification number.

- Subject names.

- Examination scores.

- Assignment scores.

- Final average.

However, it may not need the student's favorite color, hobbies, or
preferred music.

By excluding irrelevant information, the system becomes simpler and more
focused.

**2.4.3 How Does Abstraction Work?**

A basic abstraction process involves:

1.  Understand the objective: Determine what the problem requires.

2.  Collect relevant information: Identify the information that may
    affect the solution.

3.  Remove irrelevant details: Exclude information that does not
    contribute to the objective.

4.  Create a simplified representation: Organize the essential
    information into a manageable model.

5.  Check the representation: Ensure that no important information has
    been removed.

**2.4.5 Abstraction in Programming**

Abstraction allows programmers to work with simplified representations
rather than dealing with every internal detail.

For example, when using a calculator application, a user enters numbers
and selects mathematical operations.

The user does not need to understand every internal electronic operation
performed by the device.

The application hides those details and provides a simple interface.

This principle is important in software development because it allows
programmers and users to concentrate on the functionality they need.

**2.4.6 Benefits of Abstraction**

- Reduces unnecessary complexity.

- Makes problems easier to understand.

- Helps identify important variables and information.

- Supports the development of simplified models.

- Makes software systems easier to use and manage.

**Common Misconception**

Abstraction does not mean removing information randomly.

Removing essential information can make a solution incorrect. The
objective is to remove only details that are irrelevant to the specific
problem being solved.

**2.5 Algorithms**

**2.5.1 What Is an Algorithm?**

An algorithm is a finite sequence of clear, ordered instructions
designed to solve a problem or complete a task.

An algorithm explains what actions must be performed and in what order.

Algorithms are fundamental to computer science because computers need
explicit instructions to perform tasks.

For example, a recipe can be considered an everyday algorithm because it
provides a sequence of actions that must be followed to produce a
particular result.

In computing, algorithms are used for tasks such as:

- Calculating mathematical results.

- Searching for information.

- Sorting data.

- Processing transactions.

- Finding routes.

- Managing databases.

According to CS Unplugged, an algorithm is a step-by-step process that
solves a problem or completes a task.

**2.5.2 Characteristics of a Good Algorithm**

A well-designed algorithm should have the following characteristics:

| **Characteristic** | **Explanation** |
|----|----|
| Input | It accepts zero or more input values, when required. |
| Output | It produces at least one meaningful result. |
| Definiteness | Every instruction must be clear and unambiguous. |
| Finiteness | It must eventually terminate after a finite number of steps. |
| Effectiveness | Each instruction must be sufficiently basic and executable. |
| Correctness | It must produce the expected result for valid inputs. |

These characteristics help ensure that an algorithm can be understood,
implemented, and evaluated.

**2.5.3 Basic Elements of Algorithms**

Most introductory algorithms use three fundamental control structures.

1\. Sequence

Sequence means executing instructions in a particular order.

Example:

1.  Enter two numbers.

2.  Add the numbers.

3.  Display the result.

Changing the order may change the outcome or make the algorithm
incorrect.

2\. Selection

Selection means choosing between different actions based on a condition.

For example:

- If a student's score is at least 60, display "Pass".

- Otherwise, display "Fail".

The condition determines which action is performed.

3\. Repetition

Repetition means performing the same instructions multiple times.

For example, a program may calculate the average score of 30 students by
repeating the calculation for each student.

Repetition is also called iteration.

Example 1: Calculating the Sum of Two Numbers

Algorithm:

1.  Start.

2.  Enter the first number.

3.  Enter the second number.

6.  End.

Problem: Determine whether an entered integer is even or odd.

1.  Start.

2.  Enter an integer.

3.  Calculate the remainder when the integer is divided by 2.

4.  If the remainder is zero, display "Even".

5.  Otherwise, display "Odd".

6.  End.

Example:

Input: 12

Output: Even

Input: 7

Output: Odd

Example 3: Calculating the Average of Three Scores

Problem: Calculate the average of three examination scores.

Algorithm:

1.  Start.

2.  Enter the first score.

3.  Enter the second score.

4.  Enter the third score.

5.  Add the three scores.

6.  Divide the total by 3.

7.  Display the average.

8.  End.

For scores of 80, 90, and 100:

``` math
\text{Average} = \frac{80 + 90 + 100}{3} = 90
```

**2.5.5 Why Are Algorithms Important?**

Algorithms provide a systematic way to solve problems.

Their benefits include:

- Making solutions easier to understand.

- Establishing a clear sequence of operations.

- Supporting programming implementation.

- Allowing solutions to be tested independently.

- Making it easier to identify logical errors.

- Helping improve the efficiency of a solution.

**2.5.6 Algorithm Efficiency**

An algorithm should not only produce the correct result. It should also
use resources appropriately.

Two important measures are:

- Time complexity: Describes how the amount of computational work grows
  as the input size increases.

- Space complexity: Describes how the amount of additional memory
  required grows as the input size increases.

For example, searching for a name in a short list of ten students may be
simple. Searching through millions of records requires greater attention
to efficiency.

These topics will be studied in greater detail when learning data
structures and algorithms.

**Common Misconceptions**

1.  An algorithm is not the same as a programming language.

2.  A correct answer does not automatically mean the algorithm is
    correct for every valid input.

3.  An algorithm does not need to be written in code.

4.  A longer algorithm is not necessarily better than a shorter one.

**2.6 Pseudocode**

**2.6.1 What Is Pseudocode?**

Pseudocode is an informal, structured way of representing an algorithm
using simple, human-readable statements.

It describes the logic of a solution without requiring the exact syntax
of a particular programming language.

Pseudocode helps programmers plan and communicate algorithms before
implementing them in languages such as C, C++, Python, or Java.

Unlike programming code, pseudocode does not have one universally
enforced syntax. However, consistent conventions make it easier to read
and understand.

**2.6.2 Why Is Pseudocode Important?**

Pseudocode is useful because it:

- Allows programmers to focus on problem-solving logic.

- Is easier to understand than many programming languages.

- Helps identify missing steps before coding.

- Makes algorithms easier to communicate.

- Can be translated into different programming languages.

**2.6.3 Common Pseudocode Conventions**

The following conventions are suitable for introductory algorithms.

| **Convention** | **Purpose** | **Example** |
|----|----|----|
| START | Indicates the beginning. | START |
| END | Indicates the completion. | END |
| INPUT | Receives information. | INPUT number |
| OUTPUT | Displays information. | OUTPUT result |
| SET | Assigns a value to a variable. | SET total = 0 |
| IF / ELSE | Makes a decision. | IF score \>= 60 |
| FOR | Repeats instructions a specified number of times. | FOR i = 1 TO 5 |
| WHILE | Repeats instructions while a condition is true. | WHILE number \> 0 |

A variable is a named location used to represent a value that may be
stored or changed during an algorithm.

**2.6.8 Guidelines for Writing Good Pseudocode**

1.  Use clear and meaningful variable names.

2.  Write instructions in a logical order.

3.  Use consistent indentation.

4.  Avoid unnecessary complexity.

5.  Make decision conditions explicit.

7.  Check that every possible path reaches an appropriate conclusion.

Remember that pseudocode communicates the logic of an algorithm. It does
not need to follow the exact syntax of C++, Python, or another
programming language.

**2.7 Flowcharts**

**2.7.1 What Is a Flowchart?**

A flowchart is a graphical representation of an algorithm that uses
standardized symbols and connecting lines to illustrate the sequence of
steps in a process.

Instead of describing an algorithm entirely through written
instructions, a flowchart presents the solution visually.

Flowcharts help programmers and problem solvers understand:

- Where a process begins and ends.

- What operations must be performed.

- Where information enters or leaves a system.

- Where decisions must be made.

- How different steps are connected.

Flowcharts are commonly used in software development, business
processes, system analysis, and project planning.

The Open University explains that flowcharts can represent the smaller
tasks involved in completing a larger objective (The Open University,
n.d.).

**2.7.2 Basic Flowchart Symbols**

Flowcharts use different shapes to represent different types of
operations.

The following five symbols are particularly important for beginners.

style="width:5in;height:3.24562in" alt="Flowchart Symbols" />

Figure 2.4 — Common Flowchart Symbols

| **Symbol** | **Shape** | **Purpose** | **Example** |
|----|----|----|----|
| Start/End (Terminator) | Oval or rounded rectangle | Indicates the beginning or end of an algorithm. | Start, End |
| Process | Rectangle | Represents an operation or calculation. | Calculate total. |
| Input/Output | Parallelogram | Represents receiving or displaying information. | Enter a number. |
| Decision | Diamond | Represents a condition with alternative paths. | Is the score at least 60? |

Important: The diamond-shaped decision symbol normally has two or more
outgoing branches. For a basic yes/no decision, the branches should be

**2.7.3 Understanding Each Symbol**

1\. Start/End Symbol

The terminator indicates where an algorithm begins and finishes.

Every complete flowchart should have a clearly identified starting point
and an appropriate ending point.

2\. Process Symbol

The process symbol represents an operation performed by the algorithm.

Examples include:

- Adding two numbers.

- Calculating an average.

- Assigning a value to a variable.

- Updating a record.

3\. Input/Output Symbol

The input/output symbol represents information entering or leaving a
process.

Examples include:

- Asking the user to enter a number.

- Reading a student's examination score.

- Displaying a calculation result.

4\. Decision Symbol

The decision symbol represents a condition that determines which path
the algorithm follows.

For example:

- Is the number greater than zero?

- Is the password correct?

- Is the student's score at least 60?

A decision creates alternative execution paths.

5\. Flow Lines

Flow lines connect symbols and indicate the direction in which the
algorithm proceeds.

They help readers understand the order of operations.

**2.7.5 Advantages of Flowcharts**

- Visual understanding: Makes algorithms easier to understand.

- Clear communication: Helps explain processes to other people.

- Logical organization: Shows the sequence of operations.

- Error identification: Helps identify missing steps and incorrect
  decision paths.

- Documentation: Provides a visual record of how a system works.

**2.7.6 Guidelines for Creating Flowcharts**

When designing flowcharts, follow these guidelines:

1.  Begin with a clear start symbol.

2.  Use standardized symbols consistently.

3.  Arrange the flowchart in a logical direction, usually from top to
    bottom.

4.  Connect symbols using flow lines.

5.  Label decision branches, such as Yes and No.

6.  Avoid unnecessary crossing lines.

7.  Ensure that all paths lead to an appropriate continuation or ending.

8.  Check that the flowchart represents the intended algorithm
    correctly.

**2.7.7 Pseudocode vs. Flowcharts**

Pseudocode and flowcharts are two different ways of representing
algorithms.

| **Aspect** | **Pseudocode** | **Flowchart** |
|----|----|----|
| Representation | Written instructions | Graphical symbols |
| Main purpose | Describe algorithm logic using words | Illustrate algorithm logic visually |
| Modification | Usually easy to edit as text | May require rearranging symbols |
| Readability | Suitable for detailed instructions | Useful for understanding process structure |
| Complexity | Can represent complicated algorithms compactly | Large algorithms may produce complicated diagrams |

Both methods describe the same underlying solution. Programmers may use
either method or combine them during the planning stage.

**2.8 Combining Computational Thinking Techniques**

**2.8.1 How the Four Techniques Work Together**

Decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithm design
are not isolated techniques.

They complement one another throughout the problem-solving process.

A complex problem may require several rounds of analysis before a
complete solution is developed.

A typical approach is:

1.  Decomposition: Divide the main problem into smaller subproblems.

2.  Pattern recognition: Identify similarities and repeated operations.

3.  Abstraction: Select relevant information and ignore unnecessary
    details.

4.  Algorithm design: Develop a logical sequence of instructions.

5.  Evaluation: Test the solution and improve it if necessary.

Evaluation is an important supporting activity because a solution should
be checked to determine whether it works correctly and meets the
original requirements.

**2.8.2 Complete Practical Example: Student Grade Management System**

Consider a university that wants to develop a simple system for
calculating students' average examination scores and determining whether
they pass.

The system must:

- Accept examination scores.

- Calculate the average.

- Determine the student's result.

- Display the result.

We can apply all four computational thinking techniques.

Step 1: Decomposition

First, divide the main problem into smaller tasks.

| **Subproblem** | **Responsibility** |
|----|----|
| Input collection | Receive examination scores. |
| Score processing | Calculate the total score. |
| Average calculation | Calculate the average. |
| Result determination | Check whether the average meets the passing requirement. |
| Output | Display the average and result. |

**2.8.5 Common Mistakes When Applying Computational Thinking**

Understanding computational thinking also means recognizing mistakes
that can occur during problem solving.

| **Common mistake** | **Why it is problematic** | **How to improve** |
|----|----|----|
| Dividing a problem randomly | The subproblems may not contribute meaningfully to the final objective. | Identify the main requirements before decomposition. |
| Assuming patterns without evidence | Incorrect patterns may produce unreliable solutions. | Verify patterns using available information. |
| Removing essential information | The resulting model may become inaccurate. | Check whether each detail affects the solution. |
| Writing unclear algorithms | Instructions may be interpreted differently. | Use precise and ordered instructions. |
| Ignoring alternative conditions | Some valid situations may not be handled. | Consider different inputs and possible outcomes. |
| Forgetting termination | A process may continue indefinitely. | Establish appropriate stopping conditions. |
| Not testing the solution | Logical mistakes may remain undetected. | Test normal, boundary, and unusual inputs. |
| Making solutions unnecessarily complicated | The solution becomes difficult to understand and maintain. | Use the simplest approach that satisfies the requirements. |

Key principle: A good computational solution should be understandable,
logically correct, sufficiently complete, and appropriate for the
problem being solved.

**2.9 Chapter Summary**

Computational thinking is a systematic approach to analyzing problems
and developing logical solutions. It is an essential skill in computer
science and can also be applied to everyday situations.

This chapter introduced four fundamental computational thinking
techniques:

1\. Decomposition

Decomposition involves breaking complex problems into smaller,
manageable subproblems. It makes problems easier to understand,
organize, and solve.

2\. Pattern Recognition

Pattern recognition involves identifying similarities, repetitions, and
relationships. It helps people develop reusable solutions and solve
similar problems more efficiently.

3\. Abstraction

Abstraction involves focusing on essential information while ignoring
irrelevant details. It reduces complexity and helps create simplified
representations of problems.

4\. Algorithm Design

An algorithm is a finite sequence of clear and logical instructions used
to solve a problem or complete a task.

Two common methods of representing algorithms are:

- Pseudocode: Uses structured, human-readable instructions to describe
  an algorithm.

- Flowcharts: Uses standardized graphical symbols to illustrate the
  sequence of operations and decisions.

The four techniques work together to transform complex problems into
manageable solutions.

Finally, solutions should be evaluated and tested to ensure that they
are correct, understandable, and appropriate for the original problem.

The most important takeaway: Computational thinking is not simply about
writing programs. It is about learning how to understand problems,
organize information, recognize patterns, simplify complexity, and
develop systematic solutions.

**2.12 Glossary of Key Terms**
